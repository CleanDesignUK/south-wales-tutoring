Why GCSE Physics Equations Feel Hard (and How to Improve)

Help for GCSE Physics students struggling with equations, units and rearranging formulae, with a clear worked example and practical revision advice.

South Wales Tutoring  ·  11 November 2026  ·  5 min read

In this guide

  1. Understand the relationship
  2. Worked example
  3. Check units
  4. Choose equations
  5. Get suitable support

Physics can feel confusing when students know an equation but do not know when to use it. For GCSE learners, it helps to connect the physical idea, the diagram, the measured quantities and the units before substituting numbers.

1. Give the equation physical meaning

Consider the relationship force = mass × acceleration. Rather than starting with a triangle to memorise, ask what each quantity tells you. Mass describes how much matter the object has; acceleration tells you how quickly its velocity changes. The equation connects these to the resultant force.

A tutor can then ask how the force would change if the acceleration doubled but the mass stayed the same. That question checks understanding before any arithmetic begins.

2. Use a repeatable worked method

Suppose a 2 kg trolley accelerates at 3 m/s². We want the resultant force.

  1. Write down the known quantities: mass = 2 kg, acceleration = 3 m/s².
  2. Choose the relationship: force = mass × acceleration.
  3. Substitute: force = 2 × 3.
  4. Calculate and include the unit: 6 newtons (N).
  5. Check whether the answer makes sense in context.

This structure is more reliable than trying random arithmetic with every number in a question.

3. Treat units as clues

Units are often the overlooked reason a calculation goes wrong. Before substituting, check whether distance is in metres, time in seconds and mass in kilograms, where the chosen formula requires those units. A student who understands prefixes and conversions will find many Physics questions easier.

Try a quick verbal check: “If the answer is a speed, should I expect m/s or m²?” This builds awareness of the result rather than treating units as a decorative ending.

4. Practise choosing the equation

Start with questions where the correct equation is obvious. Then mix two or three possible equations and ask the student to explain which quantities are given, what is required and why their choice fits. Finally, try unfamiliar contexts.

If the student cannot explain their choice, revisit the physical concept or draw a sketch. Repetition without understanding can make a familiar worksheet look easy while leaving exam problems difficult.

Welsh students might be taking a WJEC Physics qualification or a combined science route. The exact course affects content and question format.
